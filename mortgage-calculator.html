How the mortgage calculator works

This free mortgage calculator applies the standard amortization formula that every bank and lender uses. It takes your loan amount — the home price minus your down payment — spreads it across the number of months in your chosen term, and factors in your annual interest rate. The result is a fixed monthly principal-and-interest (P&I) payment, along with the total interest you will pay across the whole loan.

Because the math runs instantly in your browser, you can change any figure and watch every number update in real time. That makes it easy to compare a 30-year loan against a 15-year loan, or to see exactly how a bigger down payment changes your monthly cost.

What is included in your payment (PITI)

Your true monthly housing cost is often called PITI: Principal, Interest, Taxes and Insurance. This calculator focuses on the principal and interest — the part set by your loan amount, rate and term. Property taxes, homeowners insurance and private mortgage insurance (PMI) vary by location and lender, so treat the result here as your baseline loan cost and add those items on top when you budget.

  • Principal — the loan balance you pay back.
  • Interest — what the lender charges to lend you the money.
  • Taxes — local property taxes, billed yearly or into escrow.
  • Insurance — homeowners insurance, plus PMI if you put down less than 20%.

How much house can you afford?

Lenders typically like your total housing cost to stay under about 28% of your gross monthly income, and all your debts combined under 36% (the 28/36 rule). A common rule of thumb is that a home priced at three to four times your annual income is realistic. Use the calculator to work backwards: adjust the price until the monthly payment fits comfortably inside your budget.

Tips to lower your monthly payment

  • Increase your down payment to shrink the loan and avoid PMI at 20%.
  • Shorten the term — a 15-year loan raises the monthly payment but slashes total interest.
  • Improve your credit score before applying to unlock a lower rate.
  • Compare lenders — even a 0.25% rate difference adds up over decades.
